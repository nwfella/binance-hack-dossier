1Executive summary

On the afternoon of Thursday, 29 October 2026, cryptocurrency began leaving hot wallets controlled by Binance, the world's largest exchange by volume. Within roughly twelve minutes the attacker had swept eleven wallets across Ethereum, Optimism, Arbitrum, Avalanche, Bitcoin and Tron. By late evening, third-party analysts tracking the flows put the total at between $5.3 billion and $5.9 billion — which, if it holds, makes this the largest cryptocurrency theft ever recorded, at roughly four times the February 2025 Bybit hack (~$1.5 billion, attributed to North Korea's Lazarus group).

Binance's public position moved in three stages over the course of the evening: first that it was "aware of reports of a potential security issue and actively investigating" with no confirmed impact to user funds; then, hours later, suspending all deposits and withdrawals while security checks ran; and finally, in a statement posted just after midnight UTC on 30 October, co-founder Yi He confirming that "user funds are safe", that Binance will cover all hacking losses, and that a full post-mortem would follow the investigation. Former CEO Changpeng Zhao, who remains a majority shareholder, was blunter: "It does look like a hack on Binance."

The single most important technical finding

The attacker did not exploit a bug in Ethereum, in a smart contract, or in Binance's code. They obtained Binance 14's private key — the key to one of the exchange's largest and busiest payment wallets — and used it to sign an ordinary-looking EIP-7702 delegation that permanently re-pointed the wallet at attacker-deployed bytecode. From that moment the wallet itself was the weapon: every subsequent deposit into it was forwarded out automatically, and Binance's own routine signing activity kept flowing from the same address while it did. One leaked signature, persistent code-level control.

Two things distinguish this from a normal exchange breach and make it worth studying closely. First, the drained wallets are the same wallets Binance publishes in its monthly proof-of-reserves disclosure as backing customer assets — CoinDesk's review of Binance's own reserve data placed the exchange's ether backing about 6% short until the stolen assets are replaced. Second, the attack pattern is generic: any EVM address whose key has ever leaked is now a candidate, EIP-7702 has been live only since the May 2025 Pectra upgrade, and the "nonce spray" technique used here defeats the usual defence of not knowing a victim's current transaction counter.

Read this report with the caveat it deserves

This is a first-24-hours document. Binance has not published a post-mortem, has not confirmed a final loss figure, has not attributed the attack, and has not said when withdrawals resume. Every number below is tagged with who produced it. Where sources disagree, the disagreement is shown rather than averaged away.

Why the "number" keeps changing

Three different quantities are being reported under the same word, lost:

  • Gross outflow — everything that moved from compromised wallets. The largest figures (~$5.8B, Arkham; $5.9B, Chainalysis) are gross, and may include transfers that were not attacker-initiated.
  • Attacker-controlled net — gross minus what issuers froze. Roughly $2.8B+ frozen against ~$5.7B gross implies ~$2.9B still moving, an inference no one has confirmed independently.
  • Binance's actual loss — what the exchange must replace from its own balance sheet. This is the number that matters for solvency and it is the one Binance has not given.

Reporting that treats these as interchangeable — "Binance lost $5.7B" next to "Binance froze $2.54B" as if the second offset the first — is wrong. The freeze reduces attacker proceeds; it does not reduce what left Binance's custody.

4How the attack worked: EIP-7702 delegation

This is the part worth understanding properly, because it is the part that will be copied.

4.1 The primitive

An exchange hot wallet on an EVM chain has historically been an externally owned account (EOA): a plain address with no code. The only thing that can move funds out of it is a valid signature from its private key. Everything else — policy engines, withdrawal limits, velocity checks — lives in the exchange's own software, off-chain, in front of the signing operation.

EIP-7702 (shipped with Ethereum's Pectra upgrade in May 2025) removes that simplicity. It lets an EOA sign an authorisation naming a contract; once a type-4 transaction carrying that authorisation lands in a block, any call to the account executes that contract's logic with full access to the account's balances. The delegation is bound to the account's nonce and persists until the account signs another authorisation that replaces or clears it.

For a retail wallet this is genuinely useful: batching, gas sponsorship, session keys, social recovery. For a hot wallet holding billions, it means one leaked signature over the right nonce hands an attacker persistent, code-level control — including every deposit that arrives afterwards. Before this incident, the four known EIP-7702 abuses in H1 2026 totalled roughly $1.08M. Blockaid's own H1 report forecast that the first seven-figure case would arrive before year end. It arrived three orders of magnitude above forecast.

4.2 The nonce-spray trick

The cleverness is in defeating the standard assumption that an attacker can't guess what a busy wallet will be doing. Binance 14 was processing withdrawals continuously, so its account nonce was advancing block by block. The attacker could not know the current value. So he did not guess — he signed fifty authorisations covering a contiguous nonce window and put all fifty in one type-4 transaction. Forty-nine failed. The one that matched made the delegation live, at a moment when Binance's own routine transactions were still flowing from the same address — which is exactly what made the subsequent drain look, to a casual monitor, like normal exchange activity.

Why this matters beyond Binance

Nonce unpredictability is not a security control. If an attacker holds the key, a nonce window is cheap to cover. The only controls that hold are policy enforcement before a signature is produced and continuous monitoring of the address for code changes.

4.3 Forced lateral movement

Once the delegation was live, the collection address became the obvious thing to watch — so the attacker did not need it. Within twelve minutes ten more wallets on four chains sent funds to the same address, each as an ordinary transfer signed by the wallet's own key, placed between routine withdrawals. Two readings fit the evidence: the same operator held those keys too, or Binance's own systems (or staff) were induced to move funds to an address the attacker controlled. Binance has not said which. Notably, the Ethereum delegation mechanism cannot explain the Bitcoin and Tron movements — those chains have no EIP-7702 equivalent — which is why investigators describe this as a broader signing-key problem rather than a single smart-contract exploit.

4.4 Laundering path

On-chain, the proceeds were split, swapped through decentralised venues, and borrowed against: $60M USDT was supplied to Aave and roughly 19,762–19,800 ETH borrowed against it, converting freezable stablecoins into unfreezable ETH. Aave froze DAO-operated markets to stop further borrowing, and stressed that its contracts were unaffected and positions remained overcollateralised. The economic reality: stablecoin issuers can freeze their own tokens, and nothing else. ETH, BTC, AVAX, OP and ARB sit with the attacker and can only be recovered by the attacker choosing to move them somewhere identifiable, or by law enforcement identifying a person.

4.5 Detection performance

Blockaid flagged the drain under two minutes after the sweep — before any public report — and confirmed the key-compromise and delegation mechanism within eleven minutes, then pushed the attacker's addresses to its customer network as malicious. That is roughly as good as this class of detection gets. It still arrived after 167,464 ETH had left. Detection is not prevention; the value of two minutes of warning is that downstream venues and issuers can start freezing before the laundering spread widens.

Frozen is not recovered

A freeze blocks movement from an address. It does not return tokens to Binance. Recovery requires either a court order compelling the issuer to burn and reissue to Binance (the established Tether precedent after prior incidents) or a negotiated/legal resolution. Until that happens the assets are in limbo — and Binance must still fund the shortfall from its own balance sheet in the meantime.

5.2 What remains loose

  • ~$850M in ETH observed moving between newly created wallets after the freezes.
  • 17,270 BTC reported untouched at the time of the freeze tally — the single largest unfreezable block, and the one most likely to be bridged or swapped.
  • All AVAX, OP and ARB taken: no issuer freeze mechanism exists for them.
  • Native ETH beyond the delegation's reach: once swept, unfreezable by anyone.

6The reserve-backing problem

The most consequential disclosure of the evening was not the loss figure — it was which wallets were drained.

Binance publishes a monthly list of wallets it controls and what they hold, and compares those totals against customer liabilities. CoinDesk's review of that data found that the addresses emptied on 29 October are the same addresses Binance lists as backing customer assets. On 1 October, Binance's disclosed ether reserve ratio was exactly 100%, with USDC at 108.26%. The approximately 204,500 ETH taken therefore leaves disclosed ETH backing roughly 6% short until the exchange replaces what was stolen — a position that is only cured by Binance spending its own money in the market.

Two implications that matter more than the headline number

1. Separation failed. Proof-of-reserves wallets are supposed to be the least reachable assets on the exchange. Here they were the payment float. An exchange that uses reserve-attested addresses as hot wallets is publishing a solvency claim about accounts it is simultaneously spending from.

2. The shortfall is a market event, not just an accounting one. If Binance must buy ~204,500 ETH to restore its disclosed ratio, that is real spot demand against a market already falling on the news. How and when it does so is a genuine open question — and a reason to watch Binance's on-chain refill behavior rather than its statements.

This is also why "user funds are safe" and "Binance will cover all losses" deserve to be read precisely. Both statements can be true while the exchange's disclosed reserves are temporarily underwater. What makes customers whole is not the promise; it is Binance's ability to fund the replacement, which depends on balance-sheet capacity that is not publicly audited in real time. The honest posture for a depositor is to treat the pledge as credible-but-unverified and to plan around the withdrawal queue rather than the press release.

6.1 The reflexivity problem with BNB

BNB is both a tradable asset and a claim on the exchange whose solvency is in question. BNB fell roughly 6–9% on the day — worse than BTC or ETH — which is the market pricing exactly that double exposure. Comparisons to FTX's FTT are intellectually sloppy (BNB is not a pure internal IOU, Binance is a profitable going concern, and there is no evidence of misappropriated customer funds) but the direction of the reflexivity is real and it is why BNB underperformed.

7Response: Binance, issuers, DeFi, law enforcement

7.1 Binance — sequence and significance

The messaging arc is itself informative. From ~20:30 to ~23:00 UTC Binance maintained a narrow statement about "reports of a potential security issue" with no confirmed impact to user funds, even as CZ was publicly saying it looked like a hack. Only after the last hot wallet drained did the exchange suspend all deposits and withdrawals. Note that the full halt came after the funds were already gone: withdrawal pauses protect a remaining balance, they do not recover a swept one, and in this case the sweeper was configured to forward inbound transfers — so leaving deposits open was actively harmful while the delegation stood.

  • 23:22 UTC — disputed the $1.8B figure publicly, asserting $2.54B frozen. Unusual and, in this narrow sense, helpful: it put a falsifiable number on the record.
  • 00:04 UTC — Yi He: funds safe, all losses covered, full post-mortem to follow, "rely only on official Binance channels".
  • Anti-phishing warning issued in the first statement — appropriate, and the single most useful thing Binance said all evening for ordinary users.
  • Not yet said: the loss figure, the root cause, the key-generation and custody architecture involved, whether reserve wallets and hot wallets are still shared, and when withdrawals resume per network.

7.2 Stablecoin issuers

Tether, Circle, Ripple, World Liberty Financial and Paxos all froze within hours, collectively >$2.6B by some tallies and $2.54B by Binance's own count. This is the most effective cross-industry response in the history of exchange breaches — and it is also an uncomfortable demonstration of centralised power: five private companies decided, in a few hours and at scale, that specific addresses could no longer transact. Whatever one thinks of that power, the practical lesson is that stablecoin holdings are freezeable by design and native assets are not. An attacker who understands this converts to ETH immediately; the operator here was doing exactly that when Aave markets were frozen.

7.3 DeFi

  • Aave froze all DAO-operated V3 and V4 markets as a precaution. Contracts unaffected, positions overcollateralised, withdrawals and repayments still permitted. This stopped the attacker from borrowing further against laundered collateral.
  • Arbitrum Security Council executed an on-chain freeze of ~$30.5M — a governance body using emergency powers against an address, which will be cited in future debates about L2 admin keys.
  • DEX venues processed the attacker's swaps. There is no general mechanism to reverse a completed DEX trade.

7.4 Law enforcement

Circle explicitly cited coordination with law enforcement; Binance cited working with law enforcement and freezes. No agency has issued a public statement, named a suspect, or attributed the attack. Expect FBI/DOJ engagement given the size and US nexus (USDT/USDC freezes), and expect OFAC sanctions on addresses only if attribution firms converge — historically a multi-week process, not a multi-hour one.

7.5 Context: a bad day for exchange security

Polymarket suspended trading the same day after a third-party vendor breach led to user losses. Two unrelated incidents on one day is not proof of a coordinated campaign, but it is a reminder that the supply chain around exchanges — vendors, signing infrastructure, oracles, bridges — is where the leverage is. Neither incident involved breaking cryptography.

8.1 What the price action is telling you

  • This was priced as a counterparty event, not a technology event. BTC and ETH fell a few percent; BNB, SOL and XRP — the assets whose off-ramps were disrupted — fell twice as much. Markets repriced access, not blockchains.
  • Privacy coins catching a bid is a laundering-demand indicator. It is not a signal about XMR's fundamentals.
  • Venue rotation toward HYPE and TRX is a measure of distrust in Binance custody specifically, not in crypto generally.
  • No systemic contagion yet. Stablecoin pegs held (USDT ~$0.9985). No major lender reported impairment. Aave froze instead of absorbing. That is the difference between this and 2022.

The trading structure worth watching

When withdrawals are halted at a major venue, that venue's prices decouple from the rest of the market — crypto stuck inside trades at a discount (or premium) to crypto you can actually move. Watch the Binance-vs-elsewhere basis on BTC and ETH: it is the market's live estimate of the probability and duration of the withdrawal freeze. A widening discount says traders expect the freeze to last; convergence says they expect resumption. It is a cleaner sentiment gauge than the spot price.

9Best practices

Ordered by how soon they matter. Section 9.1 is for anyone with a Binance account right now; the rest is durable practice.

9.1 Immediately, if you have funds on Binance

  1. Do not rush a withdrawal to an unverified destination. The most common way people lose money during an exchange incident is a panicked transfer to a wrong network or a mistyped address. Stuck-in-queue is recoverable; sent-to-the-wrong-chain usually is not.
  2. Confirm network support before you send. Binance's own status page was showing Bitcoin and Ether withdrawal routes open while the exchange's broader statement said all deposits and withdrawals were suspended. The statement is authoritative; per-network indicators lag. If a route is marked "wallet maintenance" or "hot wallet being refilled", treat it as closed.
  3. Send a test amount first. One extra fee buys protection against a typo, a wrong chain, or a destination that doesn't support the asset. This is not the moment to optimise fees.
  4. Assume you are now a phishing target — because you are. Breach events produce a reliable wave of fake "Binance recovery", "security verification", "unlock your withdrawal" emails, SMS, DMs and paid-ad search results. Binance will not DM you first, will not ask for your password, 2FA code or seed phrase, and will not ask you to "verify" to release funds. Navigate by typing the domain or using the app — never through a link in a message.
  5. Beware fake support on social media. Replies offering to "escalate your case" from accounts with Binance logos and slightly wrong handles are the highest-yield scam of the next 72 hours.
  6. Don't publish screenshots of balances, transaction IDs, or account pages. They carry account identifiers and make you a better-targeted phishing mark.
  7. Check where your assets actually sit. Spot balance, Earn/staking, futures margin, Binance Alpha and Simple Earn are different claims with different unwind mechanics under stress. Know which you're in before you need to move.
  8. If withdrawals are closed, do not chase it with leverage. Opening a hedge you can't close on the same venue deepens exposure to the thing you're worried about.

9.2 Durable counterparty hygiene

  • Size your exchange balance to your trading need, not your net worth. An exchange balance is an unsecured claim on a private company. Keep the working float there; keep savings in self-custody.
  • Diversify venues. Not because other exchanges are safer in the abstract, but because a halt at one venue should not stop you from trading or exiting.
  • Read proof-of-reserves disclosures for the right thing. After this incident, the question is no longer "what is the ratio" but "are the attested addresses also the operating hot wallets?" A 100% ratio is worthless if the reserve addresses sign daily payments.
  • Treat stablecoins as freezeable. USDT and USDC can be blacklisted at the contract level by their issuers. That protects you in a theft; it is also a reminder that they are permissioned instruments, not bearer assets.
  • Pre-stage your exit. Know, in advance, which network you'd withdraw on, to which address, and have that address already whitelisted and tested. Deciding this during a halt is how mistakes happen.

9.3 Self-custody and DeFi users — the EIP-7702 checklist

EIP-7702 changed what an EOA is. A wallet you control can now be running someone else's code without your knowledge if you ever signed a malicious delegation, or if a dApp you interacted with tricked you into one. Practical hygiene:

  • Check your addresses for delegation. Etherscan surfaces delegated accounts, and revocation tools (Revoke.cash and equivalents) now include 7702 delegations alongside token approvals. Any delegation you did not deliberately create is an emergency.
  • Clear unknown delegations before anything else. Clear the delegation first, then rotate keys, then move funds. Moving funds out of a still-delegated wallet can be intercepted by the delegated code.
  • Never sign an authorisation you can't read. A 7702 authorisation is a durable grant of code execution over your account. It is categorically more dangerous than a token approval.
  • Separate keys by purpose. A hot trading key, a DeFi key, and a savings key should be different keys in different wallets. One compromise should cost you one bucket.
  • Revoke approvals on a schedule. Quarterly, and immediately after any protocol you've used is exploited.
  • Use hardware signing for anything above your pain threshold, and verify the destination address on the device screen, not on the computer's display.

9.4 If you operate wallets that hold other people's money

This is the section that would have prevented the incident.

  • Treat EIP-7702 authorisations as privileged operations — the same approval tier as a cold-wallet transfer, not as a user convenience. Route them through policy review.
  • Enforce policy before the signature exists. Pre-sign simulation of the resulting state change, destination allowlists, and amount anomaly detection against a rolling 30-day baseline. Post-hoc monitoring is an incident-response tool, not a control.
  • Monitor every hot-wallet address for code changes, and keep a signed revocation transaction prepared in advance so clearing a delegation is one broadcast away.
  • Hold no inventory in an address whose delegation status is unknown.
  • Do not co-locate reserve or proof-of-reserves inventory with payment float. Use separate addresses, separate keys, separate signing infrastructure. This single change would have capped the loss at the working balance of one wallet.
  • Cap the blast radius of any single key. If one key can move $2.5B, that key is the control. Withdrawal hot wallets should hold hours of float, not months.
  • Assume key compromise, not key secrecy. Design so a leaked key costs you a bounded amount, detected in minutes, rather than an unbounded amount detected after the fact.
  • Nonce windows defeat nonce unpredictability. Don't rely on an attacker being unable to guess your transaction counter.
  • Have a freeze playbook with issuers. The difference between a $5.7B theft and a $2.9B theft tonight was phone numbers that existed before the incident. Pre-negotiate escalation paths with Tether, Circle and the other issuers of every stablecoin you hold.
  • Publish the post-mortem. The industry learns from these only if the details are public; silence converts one incident into many.

A note on "recovery services"

After every large theft, a market appears for people who claim they can recover stolen funds for a fee. For an individual user, there is nothing to recover from this incident unless Binance fails to honour its coverage pledge — in which case the route is Binance support, then a regulator or court, not a paid stranger on Telegram. Legitimate forensic firms (Chainalysis, TRM, Elliptic) work for exchanges, issuers and law enforcement, not for retail claimants.

The question that will decide this incident's real severity

Not "how much was stolen" — that number is already known within a few percent. It is "can Binance fund the replacement without impairing customer withdrawals, and does it resume them fully and on a disclosed timetable?" A full, prompt, staged resumption makes this an expensive but survivable security failure. A prolonged or partial one makes it a solvency event, and everything else in this report becomes secondary.

11.1 Provisional assessment

On the evidence available: the technical story is clear and unusually well documented on-chain; the financial story is not yet clear; attribution is absent; and the industry response — issuer freezes, Aave markets frozen, Arbitrum council action, detection in under two minutes — was materially better than in any prior comparable incident. The failure was not detection or response. It was architecture: one key, in one hot wallet, that also happened to be attested reserve inventory, with no pre-signature policy standing between it and $2.5 billion.

12Methodology, sources, disclaimer

12.1 Method

This report was compiled from primary reporting and on-chain analysis published between 29 October 2026, 20:00 UTC and 30 October 2026, 01:45 UTC, cross-checked across outlets. Where independent parties published conflicting figures, both are shown with attribution rather than reconciled into a single number (Table 2). Claims sourced only to social media are labelled as such. Transaction hashes and addresses were taken from Blockaid's incident report and cross-referenced against Etherscan where possible; they are reproduced so a reader can verify rather than trust.

Two structural biases are worth stating. First, early figures skew high: gross outflow tallies from on-chain analytics firms capture everything that moved, including transfers later found not to be attacker-initiated, and they are revised down. Second, company statements skew reassuring: "no confirmed impact to user funds" was Binance's position for hours while funds were demonstrably leaving its wallets. Neither bias is unique to this incident; both are visible in every comparable one.
